What Should a Founder Never Hand to AI in Their Own Voice?

Never hand AI the messages where your name is the proof you meant it. That means apologies, condolences, negotiations, and personal notes to people who trust you. AI can help you prepare to write them. You write them.

Almost everything else in a founder's inbox is fair game for drafting help. These four are not, because the person reading is not looking for information. They are looking for you.

1. Why does your signature change what AI should write?

A message from a founder carries more than its words. It carries proof that the founder spent time on it.

In February 2023, Vanderbilt's Peabody College sent students a condolence email after the Michigan State shooting. A note at the bottom said the text was paraphrased from ChatGPT. Students were upset, and the college's own follow-up email called the choice poor judgement. The college said it stood behind the message itself. The objection was to who wrote it.

The words were not the problem. The absence of the writer was.

A founder's version of that mistake is smaller and more personal. The people reading know your name.

2. Why should you write your own apologies?

An apology works because it costs something. You admit a specific thing, to a specific person, in your own words. An AI draft removes the cost, and the apology starts to read like a statement.

There is a second reason. You have to decide what you are accountable for. AI does not know what happened, what you owe, or what you are willing to fix. It fills the gap with generic regret, and generic regret reads as avoidance.

List the facts first: what happened, what it cost the person, what you will do about it. Then write the message yourself. Keep it short.

3. Can AI write a condolence message for you?

No. Condolences are the shortest messages you will send and the ones people keep. Two or three sentences in your own words do more than a polished paragraph.

Name the person. Name one specific thing you remember about them or about their loss. Say you are thinking of them.

AI has nothing true to add here. It has not met them. If you have no specific memory, say so plainly: "I am so sorry. I am thinking of you." That is enough.

4. Can AI negotiate for you?

AI can prepare you. It should not speak for you.

Use it before the conversation. List what each side wants, find the points where you can give ground, rank what you will not move on, and pressure-test your offer. Then write and send the messages yourself.

Every concession is a commitment in your name. A draft that sounds firm or friendly may agree to something you never decided. Read every number and every date before it leaves your hands. If a contract is involved, have an attorney review it.

5. What about personal notes to clients, partners, and your team?

Any message that depends on shared history belongs to you. Declining a long-time client. Ending a partnership. Correcting someone on your team. Thanking someone who stood by you when things were slow.

AI does not have the history. It writes a message that fits any relationship, which means it fits none of yours.

Routine messages are different. Scheduling, confirmations, order updates, and meeting recaps are good work for AI, as long as you read them before they go out.

6. What can AI still do for these messages?

A lot, as long as it works before and after you, not instead of you.

Before you write, it can organize the facts, build a timeline, and list what the other person is likely to need from you. After you write, it can read your draft and flag anything unclear, missing, or harsher than you intended.

The rule is simple. AI reads your draft. It does not write it. Deciding where AI stops and you start is the core of the human-in-the-loop work I do with small businesses.

7. What if you already sent an AI-written message you should have written yourself?

Follow up in your own words. Keep it short and plain. Say you should have written the first message yourself, say what you meant, and stop. Do not over-explain.

A short note from you does what the first message could not. It arrives as you.

Frequently asked questions

Should a founder ever use AI to write an apology?

Use AI to organize the facts of what happened, but write the apology yourself. An apology only works when the person reading it can tell you decided what to admit and what to fix.

Is it okay to use AI to write a condolence message?

No. Write two or three sentences yourself and name the person. A short message in your own words does more than a polished one you did not write.

Can AI negotiate on behalf of a founder?

AI can help you prepare by listing priorities, limits, and the other side's likely goals. You should write and send the messages yourself, because every concession is a commitment made in your name.

What should I do if I already sent an AI-written message that should have been personal?

Send a short follow-up in your own words. Say you should have written the first message yourself, say what you meant, and stop.

Which messages are safe to let AI draft?

Routine messages such as scheduling, confirmations, order updates, and meeting recaps are safe to draft with AI, as long as you read them before they go out.

Your name is the one thing AI cannot supply. Put it only on words you wrote.
